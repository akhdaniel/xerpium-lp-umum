10 Best ERP Solutions for Garment Operations

November 12, 2026

Garment manufacturing fails quietly. A cutting batch runs short because fabric consumption was estimated instead of calculated. A sewing line sits idle waiting for trims that were never purchased against the actual bill of material. Finished goods ship late because defects discovered at final inspection send entire bundles back through repair. Meanwhile, finance closes the month with a WIP figure nobody trusts, because production tracked units in spreadsheets while accounting tracked rupiah in a separate ledger.

The root cause is rarely a lack of effort. It is disconnection. When cutting, sewing, quality control, purchasing, payroll, and the chart of accounts live in different systems, every handoff becomes manual reconciliation. Cost per unit becomes a quarterly estimate rather than a daily number. Margin per style is discovered after the order has already shipped. The ten solutions below, all drawn from the Xerpium Garment module, address these breakpoints as a connected operating system rather than isolated tools.

The 10 Solutions at a Glance

  1. Chart of Account Garment — A six-digit garment-specific account structure so financial reporting speaks the language of apparel production.
  2. Akun Produksi & Inventory — Separate accounts for raw material, WIP, finished goods, and consumables to eliminate inventory valuation guesswork.
  3. Akun Biaya Produksi — Dedicated cost accounts for material, labour, overhead, trim, makloon, and subcontract work.
  4. BOM per Style — Multi-level bills of material per style with cost roll-up and fabric waste allowance.
  5. Production Order — Garment manufacturing orders with lot sizing, component release, and real-time WIP tracking.
  6. Cutting & Sewing — Marker planning, fabric usage tracking, line allocation, and bundle traceability.
  7. Quality Control — QC checkpoints per operation, AQL sampling, defect classification, and repair orders.
  8. Dashboard KPI Garment — Production, financial, quality, and supply chain KPIs in one integrated view.
  9. Garment ↔ Purchase — Purchase orders driven directly by BOM requirements and supplier management for fabric and trim.
  10. Garment ↔ HR — Operator hour capture, shift attendance, and payroll based on line output.

These ten were selected because each maps to a daily operational decision on the factory floor or in the finance office. Modules focused purely on reporting structure or generic integration have been folded into the entries where they create the most practical value.

1. Chart of Account Garment

The garment chart of accounts uses a dedicated six-digit structure (ga_XXXXX) built specifically for apparel businesses. It separates garment receivables (ga_120100) and payables (ga_210100) from general trading accounts, defines inventory accounts across the ga_130100–130800 range, and splits revenue between apparel sales and makloon services (ga_430100). Cost of production carries its own account (ga_510100), and VAT is handled through configured 11% sale and purchase tax codes. This solves the common problem of garment transactions being forced into a generic COA, where makloon income, fabric purchases, and finished goods all blur into unusable summary lines.

2. Akun Produksi & Inventory

This solution gives physical inventory a precise accounting identity. Raw material (ga_130100), work in process (ga_130200), finished goods (ga_130300), and consumables or trim (ga_130600) each carry distinct accounts, while production assets such as sewing, overlock, and cutting machines sit in ga_170100 with accumulated depreciation tracked in ga_170200. For garment operations, the practical gain is a WIP balance that reflects what is actually on the sewing floor rather than a plug figure. It also prevents the familiar month-end argument where trim stock has been expensed but still physically exists in the warehouse.

3. Akun Biaya Produksi

Production cost accounts break manufacturing spend into the categories garment managers actually argue about. Direct raw material (ga_510200), direct labour (ga_510300), and factory overhead covering electricity, water, and gas (ga_510400) are separated, alongside trim and accessory cost (ga_510500), makloon service cost (ga_510600), and subcontract cost (ga_510700). The distinction between makloon and subcontract matters because many Indonesian garment factories run both models simultaneously. Without separate accounts, outsourced work is impossible to evaluate against in-house production, and cost per unit comparisons between styles become unreliable the moment any portion of the order leaves the building.

4. BOM per Style

The garment bill of material is structured per style and supports multiple levels, covering fabric, trim, thread, labels, and packaging as distinct components. Each BOM carries its operational routing — cutting, sewing, finishing, packing — so the material plan and the process plan are defined in a single document. Cost roll-up calculates cost per unit directly from the BOM, and a scrap percentage captures fabric waste allowance per style. This replaces the habit of estimating consumption from previous orders, which is where most quoting errors and mid-production fabric shortages originate.

5. Production Order

Manufacturing orders built for garment work translate a confirmed buyer order into executable production. Planning supports lot sizing per cutting batch rather than arbitrary order quantities, component release controls the issuance of fabric and trim from the warehouse into production, and WIP tracking follows goods through the process in real time. Labour hours are recorded per operation, creating the link between time spent and cost absorbed. The operational problem this addresses is visibility: without a structured MO, nobody can answer how much of a 10,000-piece order is cut, sewn, or finished at any given hour.

6. Cutting & Sewing

This is where garment ERP either earns its keep or becomes shelfware. Cutting plans are built around marker plans per fabric lay, and fabric usage is tracked per cutting run so actual consumption can be compared against the BOM allowance. Sewing line allocation assigns styles to specific lines, bundle tracking follows work from cutting through finishing, and defect records are captured per bundle and per operation. The result is traceability at the level garment factories actually operate: when a defect pattern appears, it can be traced back to a specific bundle, line, and operation rather than a vague batch.

7. Quality Control

Quality control is applied as checkpoints across cutting, sewing, and finishing rather than a single final inspection. AQL sampling supports acceptable quality level methodology aligned with buyer requirements, and defects are classified as minor, major, or critical, each with an associated action plan. Defective goods are routed through repair orders rather than disappearing into informal rework, and QC pass rate is measured per style, per line, and per operator. For factories supplying export buyers, this converts quality from an end-of-line gamble into a measurable process with accountable owners at each operation.

8. Dashboard KPI Garment

The garment dashboard consolidates four KPI families. Production covers daily output, line efficiency, machine downtime per shift, target versus actual, and standard minute value tracking. Financial KPIs show cost per unit, gross margin per style, WIP value in rupiah, and inventory turnover. Quality KPIs report pass rate, defect rate, rework cost, and customer return percentage. Supply chain KPIs cover procurement lead time, fabric stock by type, OTIF delivery to buyers, and supplier scoring. Widgets drill down to source documents, so a weak margin figure can be traced to the specific order behind it.

9. Garment ↔ Purchase

Procurement is driven by BOM requirements rather than by manual requisition. When a style's bill of material is confirmed against a production order, the fabric and trim requirements translate into purchase orders, with separate handling for trim and accessory purchases. Supplier management covers both fabric and trim vendors, and supplier scoring feeds back into the KPI dashboard. This closes the most expensive gap in garment operations: fabric ordered late, ordered short, or ordered against a stale specification. Procurement lead time becomes a tracked metric rather than an after-the-fact excuse.

10. Garment ↔ HR

Labour is the second-largest cost in most garment operations and the hardest to allocate accurately. This integration records operator working hours against production operations, captures attendance per production shift, and supports payroll calculated on output per line. Because hours flow from the same production orders that drive costing, direct labour cost (ga_510300) reflects actual recorded work rather than a blanket allocation. For factories running piece-rate or output-based compensation, this removes the parallel spreadsheet that normally sits between the production supervisor and the payroll clerk.

How to Choose the Right Solution

Start with your costing gap, not your feature list. If you cannot state cost per unit for last month's largest style, prioritise BOM per Style, Production Order, and the production cost accounts before anything else.

Check whether finance and production share one data source. A system where WIP on the floor and WIP in the ledger are separate numbers will not survive audit or margin review. Integration between garment operations and accounting should be structural, not a monthly import.

Match the module to your production model. Factories running significant makloon or subcontract volume need those costs separated at the account level from day one, or outsourcing decisions will be made blind.

Confirm traceability depth before committing. Bundle-level tracking and QC checkpoints per operation are what allow defect analysis by line and operator. Systems that only inspect at the end cannot support corrective action.

Conclusion

Garment operations do not need more systems. They need fewer systems that actually talk to each other — where a BOM drives a purchase order, a cutting plan consumes recorded fabric, a QC checkpoint triggers a repair order, and every one of those events lands in a garment-specific account without manual re-entry. The ten solutions above are designed to work as one connected module rather than ten separate purchases.
